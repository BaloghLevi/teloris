01 What the join costs

Revenue does not usually leak where you are looking.

None of these shows up in a closed-lost reason field.

01

Opportunities you never saw.

The companies that opened a facility, changed a commercial director or started hiring this month, and nobody knew. That is not a pipeline problem. That is revenue that never became pipeline at all.

02

Follow-ups that quietly did not happen.

Something was promised on a call, and it was sincere when it was said. Nobody wrote it into a place that watches, and eleven days later it is a lost deal attributed to something else entirely.

03

Problems that arrive too late to fix.

A deal slowing. A margin slipping. A strategic account going quiet. Each is solvable for about a fortnight, and then it is not.

Your CRM records the pipeline. Teloris works it.

04 Against general AI

You already have AI on your desktop. Here is what it cannot do.

Teloris does not claim a better model. It runs on frontier models, and so does every serious product now. The model is the engine, and nobody buys an engine. Ask an assistant to find you ten companies and it will. The list is the easy half.

01

It runs once.

You get a list on the day you ask. Signals arrive on the days nobody asked, which is most days. A market scan that happens only when somebody remembers to run it is not a scan.

02

Nobody owns the result.

Ten companies in a chat window belong to no one. Teloris puts each in front of a named person, with a reason and a date, and knows on Friday whether anything happened.

03

It leaves no record, and it forgets.

No trail of what was known, who approved, what was overridden. And every session starts from nothing, so a person has to carry the company back into the window each time. That person is the integration layer.

A general assistant is the smartest person you have ever met, who has never worked here, cannot see anything you own, cannot do anything, is only present when you speak to it, and forgets you the moment you close the tab.

05 How we prove it

Measured against your numbers. Not against an industry benchmark.

Before anything connects, we take six baselines. Then we take them again at thirty days, ninety days and twelve months. Nobody argues with their own baseline.

  • Baseline 01New qualified opportunities per month, and where they came from.
  • Baseline 02Median days from a commitment being made to it being completed.
  • Baseline 03Open deals with no meaningful contact in the last fourteen days.
  • Baseline 04Hours per seller per week spent on research and system updates.
  • Baseline 05Median days from a problem being visible in the data to a decision being taken.
  • Baseline 06Forecast variance across the last four quarters.

Everything we publish is measured this way or labelled as modelled. There is no third category.

06 Objections

Four things you are about to say.

We already have a CRM.

Keep it. Nothing migrates, nothing is replaced, and your reps type no more than they do today. Your CRM records what your team entered. We tell you what your team did not.

We could build this ourselves.

You could. Two senior hires, eighteen months, and it depends on keeping them. The real question is whether that is the best use of your two best engineers while a competitor spends those months selling.

This sounds like a lot of trust to place in software.

The opposite. Nothing connects until you connect it. Nothing runs without an approval you configure. Every action is scoped, logged and reversible. Most companies today have less control over what actually happens, because most of what happens is recorded nowhere at all.

You are very early.

We are, and that cuts both ways honestly. The first customers shape the system and it remembers what it learns from them, so an early customer accumulates something a later one cannot buy. And the entry point costs you a conversation rather than a quarter.

One system. From the market signal to the closed deal.
